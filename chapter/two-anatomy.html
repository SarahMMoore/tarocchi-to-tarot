Tarocchi to Tarot

A History of Card Reading

Anatomy and Production of Tarocchi

British Museum playing card collections, historical studies on early European printing technologies, and Renaissance guild records of Italian Cartegiari (card-makers).

Deck Architecture

Before city-states began customizing their games, a foundational Renaissance deck was established with 78 cards divided into two explicit categories:

  • The Trionfi (The Triumphs / Trumps): 22 cards featuring allegorical illustrations (now known as the Major Arcana). These acted as a permanent, standalone suit used exclusively to overpower standard cards during gameplay.
  • The Suited Cards: 56 cards divided into four traditional suits (Swords, Cups, Coins, and Batons). Each suit contained ten numerical "pip" cards (1 through 10) and four Court cards: the King, Queen, Knight (Cavalieri), and Page (Fanti).

The Ancestry of Modern Playing Cards

It is a widespread myth that the standard 52-card deck evolved by simplifying the Tarocchi deck; historical evidence shows that the decks evolved from standard playing cards. Playing cards containing four suits and court cards arrived in Europe from the Islamic Mamluk Sultanate in the late 14th century. In the early 15th century, Italian creators added a fifth suit of trump cards (the carte da trionfi), inventing the game of Tarocchi.

The Joker is an American Invention

The Joker did not evolve from the Fool. The Fool (or Il Matto) has always been an integral part of Tarocchi. The Joker was invented in the United States around the 1860s as a specialized extra trump card for the popular trick-taking game Euchre. Its phonetic name derives from Jucker, the German name for Euchre.

Court Cards and the Missing Knight

The reduction of the court cards from four to three was not a simplification of the deck. In early card decks, the court structure was male: King, Knight, and Foot Soldier.

The French System: When French manufacturers standardized their deck, they chose to introduce a female Queen to replace one of the male ranks, settling on the King, Queen, and Valet (Jack).

The Tarot System: When Tarot was created, inventors intentionally expanded the court system up to four ranks specifically for Tarot games, keeping both the Knight and the Queen.

The Shift to French Suits

The evolution of the suits from Latin signs (Swords, Cups, Coins, Batons) to the modern French system (Spades, Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs) occurred to favor mass production:

By replacing the intricate, multi-colored artwork of the Latin suit signs with simple, two-toned, stenciled geometric silhouettes, French artisans in the late 15th century drastically slashed production costs. This economic efficiency is what allowed the French-suited standard deck to outcompete other regional styles and achieve global dominance.

The traditional suits transformed into the French system we recognize today: Swords became Spades, Cups became Hearts, Coins became Diamonds, and Batons became Clubs.

Production and Materiality: Luxury vs. The Street

The survival and style of early Tarocchi decks depended entirely on how they were made, dividing the game along strict socioeconomic lines:

  • The Aristocratic Luxury Decks: Decks like the famous Milanese Visconti-Sforza were commissioned by nobility as individual works of art. They were individually hand-painted by court artists using egg tempera, fine inks, and real gold leaf stampings on thick card stock. Because they were too valuable to be shuffled roughly, they were preserved in pristine condition.
  • The Popular Woodblock Decks: For the lower classes, card-makers (cartegiari) utilized early industrial woodblock printing. Outlines were stamped onto sheets of crude paper using carved wooden blocks, and the color was quickly applied using stencils. Because these everyday gaming decks were played with extensively in taverns, they were worn to pieces, making surviving specimens incredibly rare.

HISTORICAL NOTE:

Banning the Gamble, Saving the Art

Contrary to popular new age myths, the medieval and renaissance catholic church never officially issued edicts banning or condemning tarot cards as instruments of witchcraft or heresy. While early puritanical street preachers and municipal magistrates routinely banned common dice games and standard pip card decks to curb lower-class street gambling, tarocchi decks escaped censorship. Because they were high-end luxury items owned by ruling-class aristocrats, the deck enjoyed political protection from both state and ecclesiastical courts.

Variations of Tarocchi

Historical archives of the Milanese Visconti court, Renaissance gaming edicts from Bologna and Florence, and municipal card-playing records from 15th- and 16th-century Italy.

As the game of Tarocchi spread across the Italian peninsula during the Renaissance, it was not a standardized system. Different city-states modified the deck's structure, card counts, and allegorical imagery to suit local artistic tastes, political climates, and regional card-playing rules.

Deck Profiles

Visconti-Sforza Decks

See the Visconti Sforza - Gallery of the Major Arcana

  • Origin & Era: Milan, mid-15th Century
  • Total Cards: 78 Cards
  • Distinct Features: These are the oldest surviving luxury decks. Hand-painted with real gold leaf for the Milanese nobility, they notably lack card numbers, titles, or inscriptions, meaning players had to memorize the card hierarchies.

Tarocco Bolognese

  • Origin & Era: Bologna, 15th Century to Present
  • Total Cards: 62 Cards
  • Distinct Features: A condensed deck that removed several pip cards. Due to local political and religious censorship, it famously replaced the traditional four "papi" cards (Pope, Popess, Emperor, Empress) with four generic figures known as the "Moors" (Moretti).

HISTORICAL NOTE:

The Bolognese Papal Crisis of 1725

The substitution of the four traditional "papi" (Pope, Popess, Emperor, Empress) cards with the generic "moors" (Moretti) in the Tarocco Bolognese was the direct result of a major 18th-century geopolitical clash. In 1725, a local printer published a deck that satirized the church's hierarchy. Because Bologna was governed as a papal state, the Catholic Church threatened the entire city's guild of card-makers with mass excommunication. To save their industry, printers stripped all religious figures from the trump hierarchy, replacing them with politically safe, secular Moorish soldiers.

Minchiate de Firenze

See the Minchiate di Firenze - Gallery of the Major Arcana

  • Origin & Era: Florence, 16th Century
  • Total Cards: 97 Cards
  • Distinct Features: An expanded Florentine powerhouse. The creators added the 12 signs of the zodiac, the four elements (Fire, Water, Earth, Air), and the three theological virtues (Faith, Hope, Charity) directly into the trump lineup, turning the deck into an educational map of the cosmos.

Tarocco Siciliano

  • Origin & Era: Sicily, 16th Century
  • Total Cards: 63 Cards
  • Distinct Features: A heavily modified Southern Italian variant. It altered the Major Arcana sequence significantly, completely removing the Popess and replacing standard imagery with unique cultural icons, such as substituting the traditional card motifs with "The Ship" (Il Vascello).

Beyond Italy

International playing-card society historical catalogs, French regulatory tax records for gaming cards, and Central European Tarock tournament guidelines.

As Tarocchi traveled out of Italy via military conflicts and trade routes, it morphed into regional card games known as "Tarot" in France and "Tarock" (or Tarok/Tarokk) across Central Europe. These games abandoned the Italian suits (Swords, Cups, Coins, Batons) in favor of French suits (Spades, Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs) and replaced Renaissance art with everyday scenes, animals, or historical events.

French Tarot (Jeu de Tarot)

  • Origin & Era: France, 16th Century to Present (Major revival in the 1970s)
  • Total Cards: 78 Cards
  • Distinct Features: This remains the second most popular card game in France today. It utilizes a modernized 78-card deck featuring a unique "Knight" court card inserted between the Jack and Queen. The trumps feature scenes of traditional French rural life rather than classical allegories, and it includes a special unnumbered card called "The Excuse" (The Fool) which players use to avoid playing a card they want to save.

Austrian Königrufen (King-Calling)

  • Origin & Era: Austria and the former Austro-Hungarian Empire, 18th Century to Present
  • Total Cards: 54 Cards
  • Distinct Features: Widely considered the "king" of Central European Tarock games, this variation condenses the deck down to 54 cards by removing several lower pip cards. The game is named for its unique bidding mechanic, where the leading player dynamically declares a hidden partner by "calling" a specific King card held by another player at the table.

Swiss Troggu & Troccas

  • Origin & Era: Switzerland (Grisons and Valais cantons), 17th Century to Present
  • Total Cards: 62 Cards (Troggu) or 78 Cards (Troccas)
  • Distinct Features: Kept alive in isolated Swiss mountain communities, these games are distinct because they still use older Swiss- or Italian-suited card patterns. In Troggu, the red or round suits (Cups and Coins) feature point values that are ranked in reverse order, adding a unique structural challenge to the gameplay.
