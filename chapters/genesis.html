Global Pre-History of Playing Cards

Core Sources: International Playing-Card Society Historical Tech Archives, Tang Dynasty Imperial Court Chronicles, and Mediterranean Silk Trade Customs Manifests.

  • Tang Dynasty China (9th Century CE) - The "Leaf Game"

    Long before Renaissance nobility commissioned painted Italian decks, the physical medium of playing cards was invented in China.

    • The Innovation: The earliest credible references to card play appear in late Tang Dynasty texts. In 868 CE, documents describe Princess Tongchang playing a "leaf game" (yezi xi) with members of the royal clan.
    • The Materiality: Modeled after early paper currency, these paper "leaves" were manufactured using early woodblock printing. They evolved into "money cards" featuring suits representing single coins, strings of cash, and multiples of currency.

  • The Transcontinental Journey to Europe

    The deck of cards did not materialize out of thin air in Italy. It migrated across continents over several centuries:

    • The Silk Road: Chinese card game variants traveled westward along major trade networks through India and Persia.
    • The Mamluk Sultanate (13th Century): Islamic merchants in Egypt refined the system into highly structured 52-card decks. Due to religious prohibitions on depicting human forms, these decks used complex geometric and calligraphic designs. They featured four suits: Polo Sticks, Coins, Swords, and Cups.
    • The European Arrival (1370s): Trade and military interaction brought these Egyptian cards to European ports like Venice and Barcelona. Within decades, local card makers adapted these shapes into the standard Italian suits that laid the groundwork for Tarocchi.

Petrarch’s Triumphs and Literary Parallels

Francesco Petrarch’s 14th-century allegorical poem, I Trionfi ("The Triumphs"), 15th-century Northern Italian court records, and Geoffrey Chaucer’s 14th-century The Canterbury Tales.

  • The Renaissance Allegory: The 22 cards we know today as the Major Arcana were originally called carte da trionfi (cards of the triumphs). Their structure directly mirrors the psychological and spiritual hierarchy found in Francesco Petrarch's celebrated 14th-century poem, I Trionfi. In both the poem and the card game, each stage represents a force that conquers the previous one, charting a journey from earthly desire to divine eternity.
  • The Shared Framework Narrative: Before modern psychology defined archetypes, medieval and Renaissance literature used them to explore the human condition. A prime literary example is Geoffrey Chaucer’s 14th-century masterpiece, The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer utilizes a classic framework narrative: a diverse group of pilgrims travel together on a spiritual journey, sharing stories that reveal their inner virtues, flaws, and motivations.
  • The Visual Pilgrimage: While Chaucer wrote completely independently of the Tarot (which was invented decades later in Italy), his literary pilgrimage beautifully mirrors what modern readers call "The Fool's Journey." In modern Tarot philosophy, the Major Arcana acts as a visual pilgrimage. The Fool (Card 0) is the ultimate, naive traveler who walks through the world, encountering distinct archetypal figures to gain wisdom and achieve spiritual completion.

Historical Note

The Custom of Tarocchi Appropriati (1500s): Centuries before modern psychology or occultism viewed the deck as a system for charting human personality, Renaissance elites were already using the cards for interpersonal character mapping. In the mid-16th century, a popular parlor game called tarocchi appropriati emerged in Italian courts. In this game, courtiers would randomly draw a trump card from the pile, and local court poets would immediately compose extemporaneous, witty verses matching the structural meaning of that specific card to the person’s actual virtues, flaws, or social reputation.
